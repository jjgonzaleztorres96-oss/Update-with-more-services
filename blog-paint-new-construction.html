Painting a Newly Built Home: What Builders Don't Tell You

If you have ever moved into a newly built home and noticed the paint scuffing from a touch, or watched a white wall turn yellow within the first year, you've encountered the standard builder paint job. Here's what's actually going on — and what to do about it.

Jay Gonzalez · September 2025 · 6 min read

What Builder Paint Actually Is

Production home builders use contractor-grade paint that is specifically selected for speed of application and low per-unit cost — not for durability or appearance. This is not a secret in the industry. The paint is typically a single-coat application of a low-sheen, low-VOC flat white that checks the regulatory boxes and makes the home look clean on moving day. What it doesn't do is hold up to life.

Single-coat flat paint with no primer on new drywall absorbs uneven amounts of paint across the surface, which leads to the blotchy, chalky appearance that many new-build owners notice within the first few months. The paper face of drywall and the joint compound used at seams absorb paint very differently, creating inconsistent sheen and colour that becomes more visible as the light changes through the day.

The Settlement Issue in New Construction

New homes settle. It's a normal part of the construction process — the framing, the concrete, and the drywall all adjust to load and temperature over the first year or two. This settling typically produces hairline cracks at corners, at ceiling-wall junctions, and around door and window frames. These are not structural concerns, but they make the fresh paint job look significantly worse as they develop.

For this reason, many painting professionals recommend waiting until the first year of settlement has passed before investing in a high-quality repaint of a new build. You address the cracks, repaint properly, and the finish holds significantly better because the major movement has already happened.

What a Professional Repaint of a New Build Involves

When we repaint a new-build home that has been lived in for a year or two, the process includes: filling all settlement cracks and seams that have opened, lightly sanding the existing finish to create adhesion profile, spot-priming any repaired areas, and applying two full coats of premium paint. This is a fundamentally different job than the single-coat application the builder completed, and the result looks and performs accordingly.

The colour story also typically changes — most new-build owners want to move beyond the builder white and personalize the home with colours that reflect their taste and work with their furnishings. A repaint is the moment that transforms a house from something that was built for a generic buyer into something that belongs to the people actually living in it.

Upgrading the Builder Package vs. Waiting

Many builders offer interior paint upgrade packages at the time of purchase — darker or richer colours, sometimes a second coat. In my experience, these upgrades are still applied with builder-grade product and builder-speed technique, just in a different colour. They are better than builder white, but they are not a substitute for a professional repaint with premium product.

If you are purchasing a new build and are serious about your interior paint quality, my recommendation is to budget for a professional repaint in year two rather than paying for the builder upgrade. You'll get a better result with a longer-lasting finish, and you'll be able to address the settlement that the builder paint can't account for.
