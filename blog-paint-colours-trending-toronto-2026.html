Paint Colours Trending in Toronto in 2026

Three Benjamin Moore colours Toronto homeowners and condo buyers are choosing most this year — what makes each one work, and where to use them.

Jay Gonzalez · November 23, 2026 · 5 min read

Colour trends shift, but the ones that stick tend to earn it. Over the past year, we’ve painted dozens of homes and condos across Toronto and the GTA, and certain Benjamin Moore colours keep showing up on our quote requests. Here are three that are standing out in 2026 — and what our clients are actually saying about them once the work is done.

White Dove OC-17 — The Warm White Toronto Can’t Stop Using

White Dove has been around for years, and it keeps coming back because it works. It’s a soft, slightly warm white — not stark, not yellow, just clean. Under natural light it reads as a true white. Under warm artificial light it feels comfortable and inviting.

We used it on a recent 700 sq. ft. condo in North York. The client wanted a full refresh before moving in — walls, ceilings, trim, and doors throughout. White Dove carried across all of those surfaces without feeling flat or cold, which is harder to achieve than it sounds.

Where it works best: Condos, open-concept spaces, and anywhere you want a bright finish without the clinical edge that pure whites can have. It also photographs extremely well, which matters if you’re listing a property.

What to watch: White Dove is warm, so it may not be the right choice if your existing trim or fixtures lean cool or grey. Bring a sample card and look at it next to what’s staying in the room before you commit.

Hale Navy HC-154 — The Accent Colour That Keeps Coming Back

Hale Navy is a deep, slightly greyed navy that sits somewhere between classic and contemporary. It doesn’t feel trendy — it feels considered. That’s likely why it keeps showing up in Toronto homes that are going for a finished, deliberate look rather than a quick refresh.

We’ve seen it used on kitchen islands, built-in shelving, powder room walls, and front doors. It pairs naturally with white trim, brass hardware, and natural wood. In smaller doses, like a single accent wall or a set of interior doors, it adds depth without making a room feel heavy.

Where it works best: Feature walls, cabinetry, and trim details where you want contrast. It’s also one of the stronger front door colours we’ve painted — visible from the street without being loud.

What to watch: Hale Navy reads differently depending on the light in your space. In a room with limited natural light, it can feel very dark. Always look at a full sample on the actual wall before painting the whole room.

Pale Oak OC-20 — The Greige That Works in Any Room

Pale Oak is a warm greige — somewhere between beige and grey, with enough warmth to feel comfortable and enough neutrality to work alongside almost anything. It’s the colour clients choose when they want their space to feel calm and cohesive without committing to a strong direction.

It’s particularly popular in Toronto condos and older homes being updated for resale. It reads differently depending on the light — warmer in the morning, cooler in the evening — which can actually be an advantage in open-concept spaces where the same wall is seen under multiple light conditions throughout the day.

Where it works best: Living rooms, bedrooms, and open-concept areas where you want the colour to recede and let the furniture and finishes do the work. It pairs well with white trim, light hardwood, and both warm and cool accents.

What to watch: On walls that get a lot of direct afternoon sun, Pale Oak can read quite warm — almost peachy. Sample it at different times of day before you commit to the whole room.

How to Choose the Right Colour for Your Space

The best colour for your home isn’t the most popular one — it’s the one that works with your existing floors, trim, fixtures, and light. Trending colours can narrow the starting list, but the final decision always comes back to your specific space.

A few things that actually matter:

  • Natural light direction. North-facing rooms get cool, indirect light. South-facing rooms get warm, direct sun. The same colour will look noticeably different in each.
  • What’s staying. If your floors, countertops, or cabinetry have a fixed undertone, your wall colour needs to work with it — not fight it.
  • Sample size. Small paint chips lie. Get a large sample pot and paint at least a 30 cm square on the actual wall. Look at it in the morning, at noon, and in the evening before deciding.

If you’re unsure where to start, we’re happy to walk through the options when we do your estimate. We’ve seen enough finished rooms to give you an honest opinion about what tends to work — and what tends to disappoint once it’s on the wall.
