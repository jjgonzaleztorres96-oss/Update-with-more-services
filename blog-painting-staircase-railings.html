How to Paint Staircases and Railings the Right Way

Staircases and railings get more contact wear than almost any other surface in a home — touched hundreds of times a day, scuffed by shoes, gripped with oily hands. Getting a finish that holds up on these surfaces is about product selection and preparation more than technique. Here's what works.

Jay Gonzalez · November 2025 · 5 min read

Why Staircases Fail Faster Than Other Painted Surfaces

The staircase and railing is the highest-traffic painted surface in most homes. Every person who goes up or down the stairs runs their hand along the railing. Shoes drag along the risers and baseboards. The corners of the treads take impact from every step. Standard wall paint — even quality paint — is not formulated for this kind of contact and abrasion, and it shows within months.

The most common complaint I hear about DIY staircase paint jobs is that they start chipping and scuffing within a season. Usually the problem isn't application technique — it's product selection. The wrong paint for the surface, or no prep, or both.

Product Selection for Railings

For wood railings and balusters, I use a hard, durable alkyd-hybrid or waterborne alkyd enamel rather than standard latex wall paint. These products cure harder than standard latex, which means they hold up to repeated grip contact without marking or scuffing. Benjamin Moore Advance and Sherwin-Williams Emerald Urethane are two products I've used consistently on staircase and railing projects with excellent long-term results.

For metal railings, the approach is different — clean, sand lightly to create profile, apply a metal primer, and then top coat with an enamel formulated for metal surfaces. Metal railings that are painted without primer or with latex wall paint peel very quickly, especially on exterior or semi-exposed staircases.

Preparation for Treads and Risers

Stair treads, if they are being painted (rather than stained or left bare), need thorough preparation. Sand thoroughly to smooth any existing finish and create profile for adhesion, clean away all dust, and apply a stain-blocking primer before any finish coat. This is especially important on treads that are being repainted over an old dark colour — without a good primer, the old colour bleeds through within days.

Working on a staircase means you can't take the surface out of commission all at once — people need to get up and down while the work is happening. We typically do alternating treads on day one, let them cure, then do the remaining treads on day two. It adds time but it's the only way to maintain access.

The Right Finish Sheen for Railings and Trim

High-traffic surfaces like railings, handrails, and stair trim should be finished in a semi-gloss or gloss sheen. These sheens cure harder, clean more easily, and show wear significantly later than eggshell or satin on the same surface. The higher the traffic, the higher the sheen that's warranted. I've seen painters try to match railing paint to the wall sheen for visual consistency, and the railing always loses — it starts showing wear within months.

If visual unity between walls and railings is a concern, the solution is to use the same colour family but at different sheens — matte walls, satin or semi-gloss on the railing. The sheen difference reads naturally as a material difference, not as a visual inconsistency.
